# Certify releases, state assumptions, name gaps

A drawing question-answering system "knows what it doesn't know" when three separate mechanisms work together: independent checks that produce evidence for or against each answer, a selector whose release threshold is certified on held-out drawings with a finite-sample bound, and an output format that says which reading and which element set an answer used, or which data is missing. The three data sources need different medicine. On Swiss floor plans the checks already exist (252 of 294 wrong answers flagged), so the work is to certify an operating point and stop over-flagging. On scanned plans (186 of 294 flagged) the missing piece is a count-level probability built from calibrated detector boxes and view disagreement. On free-text IFC questions (41% fully correct, almost nothing flagged) the failures are not computation errors but reading errors, and the literature is clear that sampling or agreement signals cannot see a consistent misreading; these need deterministic coverage and absence checks, a catalogue of definitions whose variants are all computed, and a four-state answer. The honest guarantee available is narrow: "among released answers of this type from this source, at most α are wrong, with 95% confidence, on drawings like the calibration ones". It is a statement about a population, it breaks when the question mix changes, and with a 59% base error rate on IFC free text no method can release more than about 42% of answers at a 2% error target. Most of the engineering is one to two days of deterministic code plus about twenty lines of statistics; the long work is labelled data for unanswerable and ambiguous questions and learning the reference authors' default definitions. Nearly all quantitative evidence below is transferred from image classification, text QA and text-to-SQL; nothing has been measured on this system yet.

## Three sources fail in three ways, so one confidence score is not enough

The current numbers describe three different problems. The Swiss floor-plan solver is 99.2% accurate and its checks catch 86% of the wrong answers (252 of 294). The scanned-plan solver catches 63% (186 of 294). The IFC free-text path is right 41% of the time and flags almost nothing, and its misses are a different element set or definition from the reference, incomplete multi-part answers, and answers given where the reference says the data is absent. The first two are measurement problems: the question was understood and the extraction or detection was imperfect. The third is an interpretation problem: the computation was exact for a reading the reference author did not intend.

This distinction decides which tools apply. Selective prediction, calibration and conformal methods all take a confidence score and turn it into a threshold or an interval. They are only as good as the score, and a score that is blind to the failure adds nothing. The notes' own calculation makes the point: because the existing label on the IFC source flags almost nothing, it ranks right and wrong answers about as well as a random score. Fixing that source therefore starts with generating new signals, not with statistics. A second consequence follows from the size difference between sources. A pooled metric is dominated by the easy source, so pooled selective accuracy can look excellent while IFC answers are mostly wrong. Every number in this report should be computed and reported per source and per question type.

One piece of context tempers the IFC figure. The best published system on IFC-Bench v2 (1,027 question-answer pairs over 37 models) scores **56.6%** with all four grading criteria required, and its authors list a 55 to 57% ceiling as a limitation ([Hellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01698)). Two BIM experts grading 74 answers independently disagreed on at least one criterion for **27%** of questions, and nearly all disagreements were about interpretation ([EC3 2026](https://ec-3.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/EC32026_347.pdf)). Part of the gap between 41% and 100% is unreachable under a single reference answer, and part is reachable only by matching the annotators' defaults. That is an argument for showing the reading used, since a visible assumption can be corrected and a silent one cannot.

## A certified release threshold is twenty lines of code and a careful split

### What the guarantee says and how to compute it

A selective predictor is an answerer plus a selector that decides whether to release each answer. Two numbers describe it: coverage, the fraction released, and selective risk, the error rate among released answers ([Geifman and El-Yaniv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.08500)). The procedure that turns this into a guarantee is the same in the selective-classification and the risk-control literature. Sort held-out answers by a confidence score, scan thresholds from strictest to loosest, and at each threshold compute the exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) upper bound on the error rate among accepted answers. Keep loosening while the bound stays under the target α. With independent calibration items, the true selective risk is then below α with probability at least 1−δ ([Angelopoulos and Bates](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)). Testing a pre-ordered sequence of thresholds and stopping at the first failure removes any multiple-testing penalty ([Learn then Test](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.01052)). The published track record is good: on ImageNet the method guaranteed 2% top-5 error at about 54% coverage with 99.9% confidence, and observed test risk never exceeded the bound in any table ([Geifman and El-Yaniv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.08500)).

The exact binomial bound matters because errors here are rare. In one ablation of bound families on 1,102 examples, the exact approach certified 94.0% coverage where a Hoeffding bound with a union correction certified 73.8% ([Basu, single-author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08907)). The research notes computed what the exact bound demands at 95% confidence. Zero observed errors certify a 2% ceiling only with at least **149 accepted calibration answers**, and a 1% ceiling with 299. With 1,000 accepted answers, at most 12 errors still certify 2%; with 10,000, at most 176. These figures are the notes' arithmetic, not published results, but they follow directly from the binomial formula. MAPIE's `BinaryClassificationController` implements the same idea with "precision" as the controlled risk ([MAPIE](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/risk_control/1-quickstart/plot_risk_control_binary_classification/)), though its interface changed between versions; a scipy implementation is short enough to avoid the dependency.

### Where the guarantee breaks

Four conditions limit the claim, and each has a known failure in practice.

The first is independence. Questions about the same drawing share extraction errors, so they are not independent calibration items. The only measurement found, on receipt-field extraction, put the design effect at 1.84 to 2.45, meaning the effective sample is about half the count; treating documents as the independent unit shrank certified coverage from 0.171 to 0.060 at a 10% budget ([Gurram, single-author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14639)). For this system the unit is the drawing. All splits must be by drawing, and the exact guarantee holds only if calibration uses one randomly chosen question per drawing; dividing the sample by an estimated design effect or bootstrapping over drawings are approximations.

The second is leakage. The same preprint found that refitting the score on the data used for thresholding broke a 10% budget in 95% of splits. The selector, the threshold and the reported number need three disjoint sets of drawings. Hand-written checks tuned while looking at development questions are a quieter form of the same leak.

The third is feasibility. When the base error rate μ exceeds the target α, any distribution-free method must abstain on at least (μ − α)/(1 − α) of inputs ([Kotte, single-author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29054)). Applied by the notes to the IFC free-text source, μ = 0.59 and α = 0.02 give a minimum abstention rate of 58%, so coverage cannot exceed about 42% even with a perfect selector. A 98% claim on that source means answering a small minority. A looser per-source target, or a different product ("answered with a stated assumption", described below), is more useful there.

The fourth, and the most serious, is distribution shift. Every one of these guarantees assumes that future drawings and questions are exchangeable with the calibration ones. In a stress test across 16 cross-dataset transfers, a static conformal threshold violated its target on **14 of 16** ([Kotte](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29054)); that is one preprint on language-model outputs, but the authors of conformal factuality make the same caveat about their own method ([Mohri and Hashimoto](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10978)). A hidden test set with new question types is exactly this situation. The guarantee should be stated for the familiar stratum only, and unfamiliar inputs routed to a separately reported, uncertified bucket.

### What conformal prediction adds, and what it does not

Split conformal prediction produces a set or interval that contains the truth with probability at least 1−α, averaged over calibration and test draws ([Angelopoulos and Bates](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)). That is a different product from an accept-or-abstain decision. A conformal interval on relative error says "the true value lies within ±q of the reported value in 98% of cases"; it does not say which answers are the bad 2%. The tutorial states plainly that selective accuracy cannot be controlled with conformal risk control, because accuracy among accepted items is not monotone in the cutoff, and handles it with Learn then Test instead. Abstaining on wide intervals and then quoting the original coverage for what remains is invalid.

The coverage is also marginal, and "can still be satisfied even if all errors happen in one group". Computing a separate quantile per pre-declared group (the Mondrian construction) repairs this for known groups with enough data. Even so, conditioning thresholds on groups is not a free win: in the receipt study it helped on one corpus and did not replicate on others. Conformal intervals earn their place in this system in one spot, the range shown for an uncertain detector count, covered below.

| Method | What it states | Kind of guarantee | Fit for this system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Split conformal | Truth inside the interval in at least 1−α of cases | Marginal, on average | Range for counts and measured quantities |
| Mondrian conformal | The same within each pre-declared group | Per group | Per source and question type, if groups are large |
| Conformal risk control | Expected loss at most α | In expectation; needs a monotone loss | One-sided count bounds |
| Learn then Test, SGR | Selective risk at most α with probability 1−δ | High probability | The release threshold |
| Calibration (Platt, isotonic) | Score reads as a probability | None; preserves ranking | Only if a probability is shown or a cost rule is used |

Calibration deserves its limited place in that table. Platt and isotonic calibration preserve score order, and order is what decides which answers are released, so calibration cannot repair a selector that ranks badly. The certified threshold needs only the ranking. A calibrated probability is needed only for a cost rule: with a wrong answer costing c times an abstention, answer when the probability of being right exceeds c/(1+c), which is 0.91 at c = 10 (the notes' decision-theory arithmetic). scikit-learn's guidance is sigmoid calibration for small samples and isotonic above roughly 1,000, fitted on data disjoint from the selector ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)).

### Building the selector itself

The reference study for a post-hoc "is this answer right?" model used a random forest on seven features, trained on 1,600 in-domain and 1,600 out-of-domain examples. At 80% accuracy it answered 56.1% of mixed-source questions against 48.2% for raw model confidence, and logistic regression was only slightly worse ([Kamath et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09462)). Two of its findings transfer directly. Training the selector on a mixture that includes a harder source generalised to a different unseen source, which means the IFC free-text questions belong in selector training, since a selector trained where accuracy is 99% sees almost no errors. And an "is this input unfamiliar?" detector used alone as the selector did much worse (37.9% coverage), because the system was still right on 40 to 50% of unfamiliar inputs. Familiarity is a feature and a gate, not the selector.

Asking the language model whether it can answer is the weakest option. Across 20 frontier models abstention is described as unsolved, model scale barely helps, and reasoning fine-tuning lowers abstention by 24% on average ([AbstentionBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09038)). The "not answerable" decision belongs in deterministic preconditions outside the model.

## Checks must be independent, exact where possible, and scoped to the answer

### Agreement is weaker evidence than disagreement

The classic experiment on redundant software had 27 teams write the same program independently; the versions failed together far more often than independence predicts ([Knight and Leveson, via course summary](https://www.kth.se/social/files/564df871f2765419e306178d/KnightLeveson.pdf)). The same effect appeared when spatial databases were tested against each other: some bugs were invisible because both engines used the same geometry library ([Spatter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12496v2)). The rule for this system is that two computations count as independent evidence only when they do not share a failure cause. A DXF polygon area compared with the area printed on the drawing is diverse, because the numbers arrive by different carriers. A polygon area compared with a hatch area under the same unit scale and layer mapping is not; a wrong scale breaks both identically. Disagreement between paths is strong evidence of an error. Agreement is only moderate evidence of correctness.

### Metamorphic relations cost nothing but a re-run

Metamorphic testing checks a relation that must hold between outputs without knowing the right answer. A survey found it had detected about 295 real faults in 36 tools and that fewer than 10 relations usually suffice ([Segura et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/110335/1/segura16-tse.pdf)). Affine transformation of geometry found 34 previously unknown bugs in PostGIS, DuckDB Spatial, MySQL and SQL Server ([Spatter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12496v2)), and inserting an object into images exposed erroneous detections on about 13% of inputs across eight object detectors ([MetaOD](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12162v1)).

Because execution here is deterministic, the notes propose relations in two families; none has been run on the project. On the geometry side: rotation and mirroring leave counts, areas and lengths unchanged; uniform scaling by k multiplies lengths by k and areas by k²; shuffling entity order changes nothing; and re-running room polygonisation with the snap tolerance halved and doubled leaves the room count and total area unchanged. The last is probably the most informative single geometry check, since an answer that depends on a tuning parameter deserves low confidence. On the query side: a count under a filter plus the count under its negation equals the total; the sum over types or storeys equals the unfiltered total; adding a restriction never increases a count. For a deterministic executor these are exact identities, so a violation is always a real defect. Paraphrase and translation relations are the noisy ones: independent re-evaluation of question-answering test methods measured the precision of a violation as a true bug anywhere from 49% to 76% ([MORTAR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15557)).

### Building-model validation rules as confidence signals

buildingSMART publishes its normative validation rules as open Gherkin files. Those relevant to quantity answers include unique GlobalIds (PJS003), correct conversion-based units (PJS001), spatial containment (SPS007), elements in two containers (SPS005), space representation (GEM002) and standard quantity sets (QTY001) ([ifc-gherkin-rules](https://github.com/buildingSMART/ifc-gherkin-rules/tree/main/features/rules)). IfcOpenShell exposes schema and WHERE-rule validation in Python ([IfcOpenShell](https://ifcopenshell.github.io/docs/python/html/ifcopenshell-python/api-documentation.html)), and IfcTester validates a model against an IDS requirements file ([ifctester](https://pypi.org/project/ifctester/0.7.10)). A small IDS file stating the pipeline's own minimum requirements (every space has a name and a net floor area; every door has a container and a width) yields element-level signals in a reusable form.

Two limits apply. None of these tools checks that a stored quantity agrees with the geometry, and the literature seen in the notes (at extract level only) reports that element quantities differ by authoring tool ([Korean BQT study](https://koreascience.kr/article/CFKO201534159150274.pdf)). That comparison is the most valuable missing IFC check and has to be written by the pipeline: stored net floor area against computed footprint, stored volume against mesh volume, door width attribute against bounding box, containment by relation against containment by elevation band. The earlier failure analysis of this project found exactly such cases, including a door with width and height swapped while its quantities were right. The second limit is that no source evaluates any model-checker rule as a predictor of wrong answers; the rules were written as compliance checks. Still, the IFC-Bench authors observe that models which had been cleaned with a model checker gave notably higher accuracy, which they call suggestive ([Hellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01698)).

The share of elements with no storey container deserves a specific mention. "Doors on level 1" silently misses an orphan door, so the orphan share per class is a completeness signal for every per-storey answer.

### Plausibility ranges only lower confidence

The research could source only a few regulatory minimums, such as an 800 mm clear door width in Finland's accessibility decree ([Finlex 241/2017](https://www.finlex.fi/api/media/statute/61314/mainPdf/main.pdf)) and a 2.40 m room height in the German model building code ([Forum Verlag](https://forum-verlag.com/fachwissen/bau-und-gebaeudemanagement/musterbauordnung/)). Neufert, the Swiss SIA standards and net-to-gross ratios could not be verified and should not be hard-coded from memory. Minimums are one-sided and apply to new construction, so they can lower confidence but never reject an answer. Their best use is unit detection: a median door width of 800 to 1,000 drawing units means millimetres, 0.8 to 1.0 means metres. Several independent unit estimators with a vote, and a top-severity flag when they disagree, are worth more than selector tuning, since a unit error corrupts every length, area and volume by a factor of ten or more.

### Tiers keep the flag meaningful

People stop reading alerts once most are not acted on. Clinical decision-support alerts are overridden 46.2% to 96.2% of the time ([Poly et al.](https://medinform.jmir.org/2020/7/e15653/)), and Google requires checks shown in code review to have under 10% effective false positives, moving noisier analysers out of the main workflow ([Sadowski et al.](https://cacm.acm.org/magazines/2018/4/226371-lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google/pdf)). This is a live issue for the product: the notes record that Swiss answers labelled Needs review are still right about 89% of the time, so roughly nine flags in ten point at a correct answer. The label works as triage and should not be worded as an accusation.

The notes propose three tiers, following how the buildingSMART service separates failures from warnings. Hard invariants (metamorphic violations, sums that do not add up, unit-estimator disagreement, zero or negative quantities) each mark an answer unreliable. Cross-representation disagreements beyond tolerance flag an answer and show both numbers. Soft priors and model-quality rates never flag alone and feed the selector score. Two refinements matter more than the tiers. Each check result should carry the element identifiers involved and be intersected with the elements the query plan touched, so that 40 orphan windows do not lower confidence in a room-area answer. And correlated checks, such as everything downstream of the unit scale, should count once. Finally, record evidence of absence: for many IFC and scanned-plan answers the honest state is "nothing contradicts, nothing confirms", which should rank below "confirmed by a second representation" even when no check fires.

## A detector count needs a probability of being exactly right

### From box scores to a count distribution

Detector confidences are miscalibrated, and the error depends on box position and size. On COCO, post-hoc calibration cut detection calibration error for Faster R-CNN from 15.3% to 4.9% ([Küppers et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.13546)). No calibration study of the Ultralytics detectors was found, so this must be measured on held-out plans, with matching rules that mirror the counting rule: one-to-one matching per class so a duplicate box on one door is labelled false, and a loose overlap threshold because a count does not care about box tightness.

With calibrated per-box probabilities, the number of true objects among candidate boxes follows a Poisson-binomial distribution, which SciPy provides ([SciPy](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.poisson_binom.html)). The mass at its mode estimates the probability that the count is exactly right. The notes' worked example is sobering: twelve boxes each at a calibrated 0.99 give only **0.886** probability that the count is twelve. Exact-count certainty for a dozen doors needs about 0.996 per box, which demands calibration that is trustworthy near 1.0. The same distribution gives the point estimate (the mode, a principled replacement for a tuned threshold) and a range. Two caveats come with it. No published paper validates this recipe for detection counts; it is standard probability applied by the notes and untested here. And it has two blind spots: objects that produce no box at all, whose rate must be estimated separately from held-out plans, and errors that cluster within a plan of unfamiliar style, which make the distribution too narrow. The remedy for the second is to recalibrate at the count level, mapping predicted mode mass to observed exactness.

### Label-free signals and their blind spot

Three signals have published support at the image level. The mean of the top three box scores separates out-of-distribution images with AUROC around 94 to 97 across four detector families ([Oksuz et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.00934)). Consistency of boxes across perturbed views tracked per-image detection quality on 91 to 94% of image pairs in the authors' own experiments ([CCS](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12871v1)). Built-in test-time augmentation merges views before suppression and so hides the disagreement; the views must be run separately and their counts compared. For floor plans, flips and 90-degree rotations are exactly invertible and cheap. None of these has been shown to predict count errors specifically, and all are blind to a symbol style the detector misses consistently, such as sliding doors drawn without arcs.

Domain rules partly cover that blind spot because they mostly flag misses: a connected plan with R rooms needs at least R−1 doors or openings, and a room labelled WC with no detected toilet suggests a miss. Published floor-plan systems use such constraints for reconstruction, not as a verification signal, so each rule needs its firing rate and precision measured before it is trusted. Counting conventions are a separate matter that no uncertainty method removes. AECV-Bench counts only swing doors with arcs and counts double-leaf doors as two ([AECV-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04819v1)); if the reference convention differs from the detector's classes, the mismatch appears as miscalibration on exactly those instances.

### What can be promised about a count

No published method gives a distribution-free guarantee that a given image's count is exactly right. Conformal detection offers a one-sided, in-expectation statement: a threshold such that the kept boxes are at least as numerous as the true objects in at least 1−α of images ([Andéol et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24038v2)). Split conformal on the count residual gives a marginal interval, though no paper applies it to detector counts. The statement that speaks to the exact-count metric is again the selective one: "with 95% confidence, at least X% of the counts we release as certain are exact". By the notes' arithmetic, 800 labelled plans can certify 95% exactness among answered images if observed errors stay at or below about 3.6%, and cannot certify 99% with any useful margin. Rare fixture classes cannot get their own certificate from 800 plans. If thresholds were tuned on the same 800 plans the guarantee is void for those thresholds, so the plans must be split.

## A misread question is found by enumerating readings, not by sampling

### How common, and how invisible

About one question in five to a structured-data system is ambiguous or unanswerable. The IFC-Bench v2 test split puts **19.8%** of questions in its "incomplete information" category ([Hellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01698)), and a study of text-to-SQL logs reports a similar share (seen in a search summary only) ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08902.pdf)). Systems do not notice. Given a question with several valid readings, the best model in AMBROSIA recovered 30.7% of the valid interpretations and chose its default reading 83 to 98% of the time without signalling it ([AMBROSIA](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19073v2)). Given a question about a column that does not exist, a standard pipeline invented the column in 45% of cases ([PRACTIQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11076v1)). Supplying related but insufficient context makes it worse: one model's wrong-answer rate rose from 10.2% to 66.1% ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/deeper-insights-into-retrieval-augmented-generation-the-role-of-sufficient-context/)).

The project's IFC misses map onto these categories directly. "All slabs versus floor slabs" is AMBROSIA's vagueness case. "Family names instead of standard types" is an ambiguous output attribute. Answering where the reference says "not in the model" is the nonexistent-attribute case.

### Which signals work

Cheap signals are weak. In a selective-prediction study on text-to-SQL, self-consistency across sampled queries, token log-probability, executability and schema relevance all reached AUROC of only 0.61 to 0.68; a judge model reading the question and the query reached 0.72 to 0.78; two judges from different providers reached 0.82. Even that ensemble supported answering only 27% of questions at 24% selective risk ([Richardson, single-author preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799)). A different benchmark found execution-based consistency the most reliable of the methods it compared ([AAAI paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/40523/44484)), so the evidence is mixed, but the structural argument is not. A model that picks the same default reading 83% of the time will agree with itself across five samples and be wrong five times. This is the Knight-Leveson effect again. Deterministic rules have the same property, and so does the project's earlier finding that self-consistency voting moved text-to-SQL accuracy by at most 0.13 points at five times the tokens ([arXiv 2606.29733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29733)).

Detection quality depends on whether the problem is structural. With explicit no-answer rules, missing columns and out-of-domain questions were detected nearly perfectly, missing values about half the time, and column ambiguity about 30% of the time ([Query Carefully](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.21345v1)). That split says where to spend effort.

### Deterministic checks for absence and completeness

Because execution is code and the file's inventory is known before answering, most absence can be decided without a model call. The notes propose five checks, each yielding a named reason; they are designs, not measured results. Concept coverage requires every content noun in the question to map to a plan slot; an unmapped "fire rating" or "U-value" is the strongest sign of both partial and unanswerable questions. Inventory coverage requires the entity kind and attribute to exist in this file. Attribute fill rate reports the share of selected elements that carry the attribute, supporting "based on 37 of 40 rooms". Filter selectivity flags a filter that matches nothing or everything, and on a zero match runs a fuzzy lookup before declaring absence, since IFC-Bench documents a level named "OK OG2" that standard lookup missed ([Hellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01698)). The placeholder filter from the earlier failure analysis belongs here too: exporters write `FireRating = 'Fire Rating'`, which the benchmark treats as absent.

Multi-part questions get the same mechanical treatment. Surface cues (coordination, a second interrogative, "each", "per", "and their") mark the parts; the plan becomes a list of sub-plans; and the answer has one line per part in the question's order, with unanswered parts marked instead of dropped. Completeness fails separately and often: the best BIM system gives incomplete answers on about 18% of the questions it attempts ([Hellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01698)).

If one model call is affordable per unknown question, the evidence favours a single judge over more samples: a different model receives the question, a templated plain-language rendering of the plan, and the inventory summary, and returns a per-slot verdict. The notes add a caution from the sufficient-context result. The judge should be asked the narrow question "is each requested concept present", not handed the inventory and asked to answer.

### A definition catalogue and a variant sweep

Definitional ambiguity is a first-class category in BIM question answering. IFC-Bench's own example is gross floor area, where ISO 9836 and BOMA yield different valid results, and its rubric scores a bare "2,450 m²" as a failure and the same number with its method as a pass ([EC3 2026](https://ec-3.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/EC32026_347.pdf)). The earlier analysis of this project's files shows the stakes: on one model, total floor area came to 2,266.7 m² as a sum of spaces against 2,727 m² from the exporter's building-level figure, and wall length differed by about 4% between stored and axis conventions.

The notes' answer is a definition catalogue, the BIM counterpart of the curated semantic model that analytics vendors use for terms like "best product" ([Snowflake](https://www.snowflake.com/en/blog/engineering/snowflake-cortex-analyst-behind-the-scenes/)). Each ambiguous term (slab, type, area, wall length, level, door count, external) gets its variants as executable plan fragments, a default, and a display phrase. Since execution is nearly free, every variant is computed. If all agree within tolerance, the ambiguity is harmless for this model. If they differ, the spread is the uncertainty signal and the answer leads with the default and states the alternative. Defaults should be learned from reference answers by running every variant over the labelled questions and keeping the one that matches most often; the observed miss on family names says the reference authors' default for "type" is the standard classification.

### When to assume, when to offer readings, when to ask

No study compares asking a clarifying question, stating an assumption, and showing several readings head to head. The adjacent evidence points one way. Clarification helps when used, but even after a correct clarification the final query was right only about 72% of the time for the best models ([PRACTIQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11076v1)). Users corrected a displayed step-by-step interpretation far more successfully than they answered a system's questions, completing about four times as many tasks correctly as with a question-asking baseline ([Steps](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07372v3)). And the BIM rubric explicitly accepts answers with disclosed assumptions for incomplete-information questions. The notes' rule follows: with one reading and passing checks, answer and show the basis; with several computable readings that disagree, lead with the default, state the assumption and give the alternative with its value; with too many combinations or readings that change the kind of answer, ask one question if a user is present; with a required concept absent, say what is missing and what related data exists. In batch evaluation only the non-interactive options exist. A committed value should always come first, because a judge comparing against a single reference is likely to mark a list of alternatives as wrong, and graders could not agree how to score a "soft abstention" that mixes partial data with "cannot answer". Whether the project's own judge penalises a trailing alternative is untested and worth one small experiment.

## Report risk against coverage per source, with the bound and the caveat

Plain accuracy rewards guessing. The evaluation should make abstention visible and price wrong answers. The notes assemble a reporting standard from the selective-prediction literature: accuracy with no abstention; the risk-coverage curve per source, with an oracle selector as ceiling and a random one as floor ([Kamath et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09462)); one pre-declared operating point with selective risk, coverage and a one-sided upper bound; and all of it on drawings never used to fit the selector or choose the threshold. For comparing selectors during development, the area under the generalised risk curve (AUGRC) is preferable to the older AURC, which over-weights high-confidence failures and changed method rankings on 5 of 6 datasets when replaced; the same authors say a fixed working point is the right metric for a deployed system ([Traub et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01032v2)).

Four additions make the report honest for this system. First, show the curve at several tolerances (exact, 1%, 2%, 5%). If it moves a lot, many errors are rounding or unit artefacts better fixed in the solver; if it barely moves, errors are gross and the selector is the right tool. Second, simulate the hidden test by leaving one question type or one source out of calibration and measuring how often the target is exceeded on it. This is the only available estimate of how the claim behaves on new question types. Third, repeat the calibrate-and-test split many times and report the fraction of splits in which risk exceeded α, which should be at most δ. Fourth, state the caveat in the claim itself. Wording the mathematics supports: "Calibrated on N drawings. For questions of type T from source S, among released answers the error rate is at most 2%, with 95% confidence over the calibration sample, and 70% of such questions are released. This does not cover unseen question types."

Abstention and ambiguity need their own test set, because the existing references rarely exercise them. The method with reported quality is controlled perturbation of questions the system already answers correctly, validated by a human at 80 to 100% per category in PRACTIQ ([PRACTIQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11076v1)): ask for an attribute never in the file, a storey name that does not exist, a bare catalogue term on a model where the variants differ, two questions joined into one. Keeping the matched answerable original measures false refusals on the same content. A 20% share of such items matches the two measured base rates. The scorecard then has a three-way grade (correct, incorrect, not attempted) with a stated penalty for wrong answers, following SimpleQA ([SimpleQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04368v1)); abstention recall on unanswerable items with the false-refusal rate on answerable ones; whether the reference reading appears among the readings offered; per-part coverage; and a reliability table giving the share correct under each output label. The labels are the system's stated confidence, and they are calibrated only if "answered" is right much more often than "answered with an assumption".

One reference point keeps expectations level. On IFC-Bench, the weak system abstained on about half the questions and was still less accurate on what it attempted than the strong system ([Hellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01698)). Abstention does not rescue a poor answerer.

The competition's scoring rule decides how much of this pays in points. If wrong answers carry no penalty relative to abstentions, abstaining never raises the score; the system should always output its best committed value and use the machinery for labels and trust. This rule is not in the material the researchers had and should be confirmed first.

## Show the basis, a range and a reason, and never a bare percentage

The research on human reliance sets the presentation rules. Explanations raise acceptance of right and wrong answers alike ([Bansal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)) unless they make checking cheaper than accepting ([Vasconcelos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)). Confidence displays shift reliance but do not by themselves improve decisions ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02114v1)). So every reliability signal must sit next to something checkable: the drawing with counted elements numbered, and a basis line generated by template from the executed plan, never written afterwards by a model. "12 floor slabs. Basis: IfcSlab, type FLOOR, all 3 storeys" carries the uncertainty information as fact, and is what the BIM rubric scores as transparency.

A few categorical states, each tied to an action, beat a number; Google's guidance warns that numeric confidence presumes users understand probability ([Google PAIR](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)). The existing Checked, Single source and Needs review labels describe what was done and should stay. The interpretation work adds states that describe how the question was read. The two axes combine as follows, with wording proposed in the notes and not user-tested.

| Output state | Trigger | Example | Stated action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answered, checked | One reading; an independent check agrees | "55.5 m². Outline area matches the sum of room labels." | Use it. |
| Answered, single source | One reading; nothing confirms or contradicts | "14 doors. Nothing else on the drawing confirms this." | Compare with the drawing first. |
| Answered with an assumption | Variants differ, or the attribute is partly filled | "12, assuming floor slabs only. Including roof and foundation slabs: 19." | Check the assumption fits your question. |
| Range | Count mode mass below the certified level | "12 doors (11 to 13)", doubtful boxes marked | Confirm the marked items. |
| Needs review | A hard invariant or cross-check failed | "Room labels add up to 54.1 m². Outline gives 55.5 m²." | Do not use until the marked problem is checked. |
| Not in this drawing or model | Required concept absent | "Fire ratings are not recorded in this model. Doors carry: width, height, type name." | Look elsewhere; no number shown. |

Several findings shape the details. Numeric ranges cost little trust while verbal hedges cost more ([Winton Centre](https://wintoncentre.maths.cam.ac.uk/news/being-upfront-about-your-uncertainty-doesnt-necessarily-mean-you-lose-your-audiences-trust-new-paper-published)), so an uncertain count is shown as a number with a range, capped at a width of about two before falling back to "could not count reliably". First-person uncertainty wording reduced over-reliance on wrong answers where impersonal hedges did not, at the cost of lower agreement overall ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00623)), so it should be reserved for answers that are at risk. Words like "likely" should not appear at all. Abstaining on unclear cases did not cost credibility in the one user study found ([Papenmeier et al.](https://api.deepai.org/publication/know-what-not-to-know-users-perception-of-abstaining-classifiers)), but bare refusals drive users away; a useful refusal names what could not be answered, the reason, what partial result exists and the next step, and shows no headline number because a number on screen will be used. The word "certain" should not be used unless the certified level is stated, and the calibration statement ("998 of 1,000 test questions with this label were right", with sample and date) belongs one tap down, because accuracy observed in use overrides stated accuracy ([Yin et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=569874)).

For counts, marking instances matters more than the number. Confident boxes in a neutral style and doubtful ones in a distinct style let a reviewer check one to three marks instead of recounting twelve doors, and setting a tapped box's probability to 1 or 0 updates the count distribution instantly. People are more willing to use an imperfect algorithm when they can adjust its output ([Dietvorst et al.](https://repository.upenn.edu/marketing_papers/389)). No study has evaluated tap-to-confirm on detector boxes, and none of these designs has been tested with construction users.

## Conclusion: the plan, in order

The research reframes the task. "Knowing what it doesn't know" is three questions with three owners: did the extraction go wrong (checks and redundancy), was the question read as intended (enumerated readings and coverage), and how often are released answers wrong (a certified threshold). The statistics are the smallest part and the easiest to get wrong; the guarantee is real only for drawings and question types like those calibrated on, with the drawing as the unit. On the IFC source the largest gain comes from changing what an answer is, from a bare value to a value with its basis and its main alternative, which converts an invisible misreading into a visible assumption. That also reaches the part of the 59% that no selector could, because the feasibility bound caps certified coverage near 42% at a strict target.

Two unknowns can reorder everything below and should be settled first: whether the competition penalises wrong answers relative to abstentions, and whether its judge accepts a committed value followed by a stated alternative. Effort figures were not estimated in the research notes; the split is by the kind of work.
