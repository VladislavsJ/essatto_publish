# Crop first, parse vectors, gate photos

The belief that tested, cheaper tools should run before any large model is right for one input and only partly right for the other. For CAD-exported PDF sheets that still carry vector lines and text, rule-based parsers (PyMuPDF `find_tables`, pdfplumber, Camelot 2.0) rebuild a schedule from its own ruling lines in about a second on CPU, at no per-page cost, with exact cell coordinates, provided the schedule region is cropped first. No published benchmark covers any open-source parser on large-format construction sheets, and in the one peer-reviewed 15-method comparison no method exceeds 90% F1-TEDS, so "tested" means tested on scientific and financial documents and every extracted table needs its own checks. Cloud document services are not the safe middle ground they appear to be: on the only test with real door and window schedules, Azure and AWS scored 100% on simple tables but 55% and 67% of cells on a complex one, against 79% for the best vision model at about USD 58 per 1,000 pages. For phone photos the evidence is much thinner. Page rectification, a packaged dewarping model, CPU OCR and floor-plan polygon models all exist, but none has been measured on photographed construction drawings, a single 12-megapixel photo of a whole A1 sheet falls below the text size OCR needs, and commercial takeoff products do not trust their own automatic scale. The practical design is therefore a deterministic front end that crops, parses, validates and refuses with a reason, and a large model that reads only small crops which failed validation and never supplies coordinates, scale or table cells. Both recommended pipelines below are designs assembled from this evidence; neither has been run on the team's sheets, and the report ends with the measurements that would change that.

## Where "cheaper and more reliable" holds, and where the evidence stops

Three findings from the research decide most of the design. The first is about resolution. The team's large sheet is 3024 x 2160 pt, which is 42 x 30 inches (ARCH E1, not ARCH D); rendered at 150 DPI that is 6300 x 4500 px and at 300 DPI about 113 megapixels. Rule-based parsers do not care, because they read the PDF's own vector lines and text. Every image-based tool does. Learned document parsers and frontier vision models resize the page before reading it: in a 2026 long-document study, input tokens for Claude Sonnet 4.5 stopped growing above 144 DPI because the image cap was reached, and capping the long side at 512 px cut accuracy from **0.517 to 0.296** ([arXiv 2609.29933](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29933), read as a search summary). An OpenRouter test found a 13.8-point loss from low image detail on another benchmark ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/insights/image-detail-low-cost/)). The research notes' arithmetic is that a model seeing 1,500 to 2,500 px on the long side reads a 42-inch sheet at roughly 36 to 60 DPI, where 2 mm schedule text is 3 to 6 px tall. Sending the whole sheet is cheap in tokens and blind to small text.

The second finding is that the reader's premise has direct, if small, support on schedules. Businessware tested 18 architectural drawings with 20 door and window schedules and scored correctly extracted cells. GPT-4o reached **38.41%** overall with truncated output that neither prompt changes nor higher resolution fixed; Grok 2 Vision and Pixtral Large invented entries by continuing number sequences and substituting common values; Gemini 2.5 Pro was best but took about 47 seconds per page ([Businessware, July 2025](https://dev.to/businessware/benchmark-how-well-ai-models-handle-table-processing-29kl)). This is a vendor blog with a small sample and models that have since been superseded, but it is the only schedule-specific measurement found. It agrees with the peer-reviewed table benchmark, whose authors conclude that vision-language models hallucinate on heterogeneous data while heuristic and lightweight methods stay competitive on specific layouts ([table-extraction benchmark](https://soricm.github.io/table-extraction-benchmark/)). Earlier research for this project found the same pattern for symbols: ten vision models got 9 to 39% of door counts exactly right on floor plans ([AECV-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04819)).

The third finding limits the premise. The same sources show that the non-model tools are not reliable by default either. No method in the 15-method benchmark passes 90% F1-TEDS ([arXiv 2511.16134](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16134)); cloud layout services lose a third or more of cells on a complex schedule; and Buro Happold's 2026 benchmark of layout detection on engineering drawings reports that models pretrained on general document datasets suffer "domain interference" and that general layout models "lack validation on engineering drawings" ([arXiv 2607.18997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18997)). The incumbents behave accordingly. Autodesk Construction Cloud reads sheet number and title from the title block with template capture areas that an admin draws ([Autodesk](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Docs-Files/files/Automated_Drawing_Extraction.html)), and Bluebeam exports a schedule to Excel from a rectangle the user drags ([Bluebeam](https://support.bluebeam.com/user-manual/menus/file/export-documents.html)). The industry pattern is "mark the region, then read inside it", which is the crop-then-parse design this report recommends. No vendor of a construction-specific tool publishes a method or an independently measured accuracy for automatic schedule extraction; figures such as ReadMyPlans' "over 99%" are marketing ([ReadMyPlans](https://readmyplans.pages.dev)).

So the position is: deterministic tools first is correct on cost, speed, traceability and resolution; it is correct on accuracy only where the input gives them exact data (vector rulings and a text layer) and only with validation; and for scans and photos it is a reasoned bet that the team must measure.

Two readings of this table matter. The learned parsers score far higher than the rule-based ones on document leaderboards, and that gap is real for ordinary pages. It does not transfer to a sheet where the table is a small ruled box among thousands of plan lines, because those models must first find the table on a downscaled image with a layout model never trained on drawings. The rule-based tools have the opposite profile: weak on unruled, messy document tables, exact on a ruled grid whose lines and words are in the file. A schedule on a CAD sheet is the second case. Their own risk on a full sheet is that the line-based table finder tries to turn plan geometry into grids, which cropping removes.

Licences are a real constraint only outside the hackathon. PyMuPDF's AGPL is acceptable when the repository is published; pypdfium2, actively released in 2026, is the permissive alternative for rendering and character boxes ([pypdfium2](https://github.com/pypdfium2-team/pypdfium2)). Licence statements for MinerU, Marker and Surya come from secondary sources that disagree, so each LICENSE file needs reading before commercial use.

### Finding the regions without a model

The published route for segmenting an engineering sheet is a small trained detector: RF-DETR reached **mAP@50 0.949** on an AEC layout dataset, and Qwen3-VL had the best F1 at 0.911 ([arXiv 2607.18997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18997)); a mechanical-drawing pipeline trained YOLOv11 on 1,000 drawings to split views, title block and notes ([arXiv 2510.21862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21862v2)). Off-the-shelf document-layout weights are the wrong tool: Google's Layout Parser did not recognise the schedules as tables at all in the Businessware test.

For vector sheets the no-training option is rules, and no source measures them. The research notes' recipe is: the title block is the framed strip along the right or bottom edge; schedules are connected components of axis-aligned lines forming dense regular grids with a heading containing SCHEDULE; notes and legends are text-dense blocks headed NOTES, LEGEND or KEYNOTES; the plan is what remains. PyMuPDF's `cluster_drawings` groups nearby vector graphics into candidate rectangles ([PyMuPDF docs](https://pymupdf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/page.html)). A vendor case study gives the failure to guard against: a first region filter clipped three rows at a schedule's edge, caught only by a second independent extraction ([Energent.ai](https://www.energent.ai/en/resources/customer-stories/facilities-cafeteria-dxf-extraction)).

Two properties of CAD exports decide whether the vector path works at all. AutoCAD writes SHX-font text as line geometry, because PDF does not support SHX fonts ([Autodesk](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2025/ENU/AutoCAD-LT/files/GUID-1202CC8A-364F-4E93-8E86-6F476CD83C72.htm)), and Bluebeam confirms such text is not searchable ([Bluebeam](https://support.bluebeam.com/articles/autocad-pdfs-dont-contain-searchable-text)). A region with table rulings, almost no extractable characters and very many short strokes has outline text and needs OCR. AutoCAD's EPDFSHX setting can store that text as hidden PDF comments ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/autocad-forum/searchable-pdfs-from-shx-fonts/td-p/6621435)), so the annotations are worth checking before any OCR runs. Layers survive only sometimes; where they do, layer names separate schedules from plan geometry for free. How often either holds on the team's sheets is unknown. Converting PDF to DXF adds nothing: no converter recovers layers that are not in the PDF.

## Grouped headers survive only as header paths, and every table needs gates

No tool outputs a header tree. pdfplumber and PyMuPDF return a plain grid in which a spanning header cell is one filled cell followed by `None` neighbours, and PyMuPDF's header detection assumes a single header row. Docling's HTML and JSON keep row and column spans but its Markdown and DataFrame exports flatten them ([Docling options](https://mintlify.com/docling-project/docling/api/options/table-structure-options)); TATR predicts spanning cells and column headers as boxes. In every case the distinction between "Door / Matl" and "Frame / Matl" has to be built in post-processing.

The earlier research for this project already wrote and tested that step on one synthetic schedule. A grid builder of about 60 lines on PyMuPDF's `get_cdrawings()` treats thin filled rectangles as lines, tolerates fragmented segments, and merges two grid cells wherever no separating edge is drawn, which recovers the spanning header. Words go to the cell that contains their centre, and each leaf column is named by joining every header cell above it. On the synthetic sheet this produced `WALLS / N`, `WALLS / E` and so on, kept empty cells in place and ran in **5 ms**; it was not run on the real failing sheet. That same test showed PyMuPDF kept a rotated header intact where pdfplumber scrambled it, and that `find_tables` dropped spaces inside cells, so cell text should be re-read with `get_text("words", clip=cell_bbox)`. Text order never enters the assignment, which is why the known one-column shift cannot occur.

The header band should end at the first row whose key column matches the mark pattern (a door number, a room number), not at a fixed row count. Columns are then stored under unique path names such as `door.matl` and `frame.matl`, with the original group and leaf text kept as metadata. Table question-answering research supports doing this in code before any model sees the table: methods that turn hierarchical headers into an explicit tree report gains of up to 20% in answer accuracy ([ST-Raptor](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.18190v1)), and a study of small models notes they struggle specifically with two-level headers when referencing cells ([Table-r1](https://www.preprints.org/frontend/manuscript/7e86af3e1976a5f3ba51c0ef426a9089/download_pub)). When a question says only "material" and two paths share that leaf, the system returns both, labelled, or asks; it never picks silently.

Validation is what turns "extracted" into "checkable". The gates below are the two research rounds' designs; only Camelot's scores are a library feature, and none has been run on real sheets.

| Gate | What it catches |
|---|---|
| Outer frame of the cropped region is closed | Rows clipped at the region edge |
| Row count equals the number of mark-pattern matches found by plain text search in the region | Missing or merged rows |
| Every word in the region is assigned to exactly one cell; flag words overlapping two cells | Overflowing text, wrong grid |
| Key column values are unique; every row has one cell per leaf column | Split or duplicated rows |
| Each column scored against a type pattern (area, mark, finish code, dimension), then re-scored shifted by one column | Column shift; an AREA column holding only "SF" |
| Second reading of the same crop by Camelot lattice, compared cell by cell, plus its `accuracy` and `whitespace` scores ([Camelot](https://camelot-py.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user/advanced.html)) | Grid errors the first reader makes |
| Normalised values parse (feet-and-inches to numbers, "--" as explicit none) with the raw string kept | Unit and OCR damage |

Two cautions from the reliability research apply. A second reader that shares a failure cause is weak confirmation: pdfplumber and PyMuPDF use the same algorithm, so Camelot's raster-based lattice is the more independent partner. And agreement is only moderate evidence of correctness, while disagreement is strong evidence of an error. Body cells are never forward-filled; only spanning header cells and explicit ditto marks are. Once the table is stored with cell rectangles, lookups by mark, counts and filters are fixed query templates that need no model, and every answer can highlight the cells it used.

## Cloud services and converters: fast, cheap, and weak on complex schedules

The Businessware test is again the only schedule evidence. On simple layouts Azure Prebuilt Layout, AWS Textract and Gemini 2.5 Pro all reached 100% of cells. On a complex sample Azure scored **55.00%**, AWS **66.67%** and Gemini **79.17%**; the two cloud services struggled with multi-line cells, unusual fonts and mixed content, and table size did not correlate with accuracy. Speed was about 2.9 s per page for AWS, 4.3 s for Azure and 47 s for Gemini ([Businessware](https://dev.to/businessware/benchmark-how-well-ai-models-handle-table-processing-29kl)). A rule-based reconstruction on a vector PDF works from exact lines and words, so there is no reason to expect a cloud service to beat it there; the services earn a place on scanned sheets and on crops that failed the gates.

List prices, read from third-party tables in October and November 2026 and not from vendor pages, are about USD 1.50 per 1,000 pages for plain OCR on all three large clouds, USD 4 for Textract Layout, **USD 15 for Textract Tables**, USD 10 for Azure Layout with tables included, and USD 10 for Google's Layout Parser ([aliteq](https://aliteq.com/idp-pricing-per-page), [Activepieces](https://www.activepieces.com/blog/azure-document-intelligence-pricing-per-model-in-2026)). Mistral OCR 3 lists at USD 2 per 1,000 pages ([Mistral](https://docs.mistral.ai/models/model-cards/ocr-3-25-12)) and Reducto at USD 0.015 per page ([Reducto](https://llms.reducto.ai/best-document-processing-apis-2026)). The accuracy claims for Mistral (99.24%) and Reducto (99%+) are vendor-reported, and the comparison sites repeating them state they did no testing ([dupple](https://dupple.com/learn/best-ai-document-processing-tools)). Mathpix is the only commercial tool with peer-reviewed standing, on the Pareto front of the table benchmark. Against these, Gemini's roughly USD 58 per 1,000 pages is four to fifteen times dearer, and a local parser costs CPU seconds.

Three gaps remain open. No source says how any service bills or downsamples an oversize page. No independent test of Adobe PDF Extract, ABBYY, LlamaParse, Reducto, Mistral OCR or Nanonets on construction schedules was found. EU data-residency terms were not researched from primary sources. Desktop converters follow the Bluebeam pattern of a user-drawn region with OCR for scanned content, and a third-party guide notes the export works best on vector PDFs with selectable text ([The Bricks](https://www.thebricks.com/resources/guide-how-to-convert-bluebeam-to-excel)).

## Phone photos: the tools exist, the measurements on drawings do not

### Rectify with a homography; dewarp only for reading

For a flat sheet, a four-point perspective transform from the detected page corners, or better the printed border rectangle, is the correct geometric model, and OpenCV supplies every step. A learned dewarper is needed only for curled or creased paper. The one confirmed as packaged and timed is UVDoc inside PaddleOCR 3.x: a 30.3 MB model, 19 ms on a T4 GPU and **about 0.87 s on a Xeon CPU**, with a character error rate of 0.179 on the DocUNet benchmark ([PaddleOCR docs](https://www.paddleocr.ai/main/en/version3.x/module_usage/text_image_unwarping.html)). In its paper UVDoc leads ten other methods on that benchmark ([UVDoc](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.02887v2)).

The limits are specific. UVDoc takes a 488 x 712 input and predicts a 45 x 31 grid that is upsampled to the full image; it assumes the whole page is visible with no fold-over; and its authors caution that small differences in OCR scores should not be over-read. Every dewarping benchmark scores image similarity and OCR on A4-style text pages. None measures whether lengths are preserved, and none uses line drawings or large sheets. A dewarped image is therefore an aid to reading text and must not be the basis of a measurement unless independent dimensions in both axes agree afterwards. DocRes offers dewarping, shadow removal, deblurring and binarisation in one MIT-licensed model, but its repository gives no speed or hardware figures and it was built for text documents ([DocRes](https://github.com/ZZZHANG-jx/DocRes)); whether its clean-up erodes thin CAD lines is untested. Google's ML Kit document scanner does detection, cropping and shadow removal on the handset, as a capture screen and not a server library ([ML Kit](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/doc-scanner?authuser=0)). Adobe Scan, Microsoft Lens, Apple VisionKit, Scanbot and Dynamsoft were not researched and cannot be recommended for or against.

Stitching several close-up photos into one sheet is the risky step. OpenCV's `Stitcher` has a SCANS mode with an affine model meant for flat scanner strips ([OpenCV tutorial](https://docs.opencv.org/5.x/d8/d19/tutorial_stitcher.html)), which is the wrong model for handheld photos, and one user reports an out-of-memory failure on two images ([OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/possibility-of-using-cv-findhomography-and-cv-warpperspective-in-the-stitcher-detail-pipeline/7641)). The lower-risk choice for a first version is no stitching: one photo per region of interest, each with its own scale evidence.

### Text height in pixels is the gate with sourced support

The one capture threshold with repeated sourcing is text size. Guidance derived from Tesseract's documentation asks for an x-height near 20 px and says accurate results are very unlikely below about 10 px ([EasyRPA guide](https://docs.easyrpa.eu/help/docs/Data-Analyst-Guide/OCR-Tuning-Guide/Evaluating-and-Enhancing-Image-Quality-for-OCR), [iDigBio](https://www.idigbio.org/node/106)); both are secondary sources read as search summaries. The research notes' arithmetic shows what that means: a 12-megapixel frame filled by an A1 sheet gives about 121 ppi and an A0 sheet about 85 ppi, so 2.5 mm lettering has an x-height near 8 px. **One ordinary phone photo of a whole A1 sheet is below the readable line**; a 48-megapixel capture, four to six overlapping close-ups, or a real scan at 200 to 300 ppi is needed. The gate should measure the median height of detected text boxes after rectification, not image dimensions.

No sourced numeric threshold was found for blur, glare, shadow or tilt. The values below are the research notes' starting points and must be fitted on the team's own images. SmartDoc-QA, 4,260 phone captures of 30 documents with OCR ground truth, is the public set for fitting blur and lighting thresholds, though it contains text documents and no drawings ([SmartDoc-QA](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01319900)).

| Check | Starting threshold (to calibrate) | Reason shown to the user |
|---|---|---|
| Median text height after rectification | Refuse below about 10 px; warn at 10 to 16 px | "The text is too small to read. Move closer and photograph one part of the sheet, or scan at 300 dpi." |
| Recompressed image | Long side under about 2,000 px | "This image was shrunk by a messaging app. Send the original file." |
| Sharpness per ink-bearing tile | More than about 20% of tiles under a fitted Laplacian-variance floor | "Part of the photo is blurred (highlighted). Hold steady, tap to focus, retake." |
| Glare | Saturated blobs over linework above about 1 to 2% of the page | "There is a reflection on the sheet (highlighted). Turn off the flash or change the angle." |
| Shadow | Background brightness ratio under a fitted value after correction | "A strong shadow falls across the sheet. Step back so you do not block the light." |
| Page not fully visible | No page quadrilateral, or a corner out of frame | "We cannot see the whole sheet. Include all four corners, or mark this as a detail and give a known dimension." |
| Perspective | Near-to-far side ratio beyond about 1.3 | "The photo was taken at too steep an angle. Stand directly over the sheet." |
| Residual curvature | Long-line deviation above about 1% of sheet width after dewarping | "The paper is curled, so measurements would be unreliable. Flatten it and retake." Reading is still allowed. |
| Scale evidence | Fewer than two agreeing dimensions, or spread above about 2% | "We could not confirm the scale. Tap the two ends of a dimension you know and enter its length." |
| Axis disagreement | Horizontal and vertical scale differ by more than about 1% | "The image is slightly stretched; areas may be off by up to X%." |
| Photo of a screen | Periodic high-frequency pattern or a bezel | "This looks like a photo of a screen. Upload the PDF or a screenshot." |
| Several scales | Scale estimates form two clusters | "This sheet has drawings at different scales. Select the one to measure." |

### OCR for drawing text has no architectural benchmark

No published head-to-head of OCR engines on architectural drawings, rotated dimension text or photographed sheets was found. The drawing-specific results are for mechanical drawings: eDOCr reports about 90% detection precision and recall with an 8% character error rate ([Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmtec.2023.1154132)), and eDOCr2 reports **93.75% text recall and under 1% character error** on its own small benchmark ([eDOCr2](https://mdpi.com/2075-1702/13/3/254), read as a search summary). eDOCr2's code is MIT-licensed ([GitHub](https://github.com/javvi51/edocr2)), but its recognisers are trained on mechanical notation. Its design transfers better than its weights: split the sheet into title block, tables and drawing area; detect oriented text boxes; rotate each crop upright; recognise with a restricted alphabet.

PP-OCRv5 is the best-documented general engine for size and speed. The mobile recogniser is about 16 MB and runs in roughly 5 to 21 ms per text line on CPU; the server recogniser scores 86.38% on PaddleOCR's own evaluation set against 81.29% for mobile ([PaddleOCR docs](https://www.paddleocr.ai/v3.6.0/version3.x/module_usage/text_recognition.html)). One user's test on a 4-core CPU timed the server detector at about 9.5 s per image against 5.0 s for mobile ([wikidocs](https://wikidocs.net/441124)). A vendor receipt benchmark ranks docTR and PaddleOCR ahead of EasyOCR and Tesseract on character error ([ImageToTable.ai](https://imagetotable.ai/de/references/receipt-ocr-benchmark-results)), which says little about drawings. Its Apache-2.0 licence was not confirmed from a primary page.

What does not need a benchmark is the checking. Dimension strings follow a closed grammar, so a pattern validator plus the rule that chained dimensions sum to the overall dimension gives a confidence signal without a model, and is likely to catch more errors than switching engines. Sheets must be tiled before detection because detectors downscale the long side, and each rotated box should be recognised at 0 and 180 degrees with the grammar deciding.

### Scale and room outlines are proposals to confirm

Commercial practice on scale is blunt. Togal.AI reads the written scale by OCR and states that "the system does NOT test the correctness of the scale"; the user must verify with two points of known distance on every drawing ([Togal.AI help](https://help.togal.ai/en/articles/15367669-setting-up-the-scale)). Bluebeam's calibration is the same two-point procedure, and reseller guidance for scans adds the longest available baseline, a second dimension as a check, and a separate scale per viewport ([NOVEDGE](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/bluebeam-tip-calibrate-bluebeam-revu-drawings-for-accurate-takeoffs)). No vendor or paper publishes an accuracy figure for automatic scale detection.

A written "1:100" converts to pixels per metre only when the true print size is known, which it is not for a phone photo or for an A1 sheet printed on A3. On-sheet evidence has to carry the measurement: dimension values matched to their dimension lines, with a robust consensus across several of them, computed per viewport. The notes' error arithmetic explains the vendors' advice: a 2 px endpoint error on a 200 px baseline is a 2% length error and a 4% area error, and on a 2,000 px baseline it is 0.2% and 0.4%. Agreement of several independent dimensions, and of horizontal with vertical ones, is the confidence measure. This method is the notes' design and has no published accuracy.

For room outlines, learned models return closed polygons quickly but not accurately enough to measure with. On real floor-plan images (CubiCasa5K) the best published method, Raster2Seq, reaches **Room F1 88.7 but corner F1 59.4 and angle F1 37.4**; on clean synthetic binaries the same family scores 95 to 99.6, and one method, PolyRoom, falls from 98.9 to 54.1 between the two ([Raster2Seq](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09016)). All are evaluated at 256 x 256 input on clean rasters. Zero-shot on internet floor plans the best IoU is 73.9; photographed and hand-drawn plans are untested. No paper reports area error. These models are proposal generators. The route that yields measurable geometry and visible failures is classical: mask text, keep wall-weight strokes, detect and merge line segments, close door-width gaps, extract closed faces and compute areas. Its failure modes (open-plan spaces, door swings read as walls, hatching) all show in an overlay. General tracers such as Potrace output ink outlines, not centrelines or rooms. For symbols, the project's earlier research stands: a detector trained at 1024 px self-reports door AP50 89.8 ([rtdetr-floorplan-detector](https://github.com/OldDeLorean/rtdetr-floorplan-detector)), and tiled inference handles large sheets.

Nothing quantifies the drop from clean raster to photograph for any of these stages. Reviewers of takeoff products say scanned sheets produce more misses, without numbers, and the "98% accuracy" quoted for Togal.AI is a vendor claim; the university study cited for it measured speed ([Togal case study](https://www.togal.ai/case-study/ku-study-togal-vs-ost)). No vendor found claims handheld photos of paper sheets as a supported input with a stated accuracy.

## Large models keep the semantic work and lose the measuring

After the deterministic front end, a model is still needed in five places. Reading text that exists only as outlines or pixels needs OCR, where small CPU models are the first choice and accuracy on SHX strokes is unmeasured. Tables without rulings, or with rulings broken by overlapping graphics, need a table-structure model. Region labelling on unusual sheet layouts needs a small fine-tuned detector or a vision model used for locating; Qwen3-VL's 0.911 F1 on drawing layout shows vision models are competitive at finding regions even where they fail at reading every cell, and one 2026 pipeline uses exactly that split, grounding the title block on the full sheet and extracting from the crop ([EC3 2026](https://ec-3.org/publication/ec32026_252/)). Mapping free phrasing to a column path, a filter and an aggregate needs a small language model once templates run out. And semantics on the plan itself stay with models or people: what a closed region is, which lines are walls, where an open-plan room ends, what an abbreviation or hardware code means (a schedule vendor notes these need a lookup against the specifications; [Nomic](https://www.nomic.ai/glossary/ai-table-extraction)), handwriting and revision marks.

The rule that follows from the measured failure modes (truncation, pattern-continued values, shifted columns) is about role. A model receives a cropped, quality-passed region together with the parser's row count and the text-layer words, and its output is accepted only if it reproduces the row count and every value appears in the region's text where a text layer exists. It verifies and labels proposals. It is never the source of coordinates, scale or cell values that code can read, and answers are computed from the stored table. This also fixes cost: by the earlier research's reading of vendor documentation, one image costs up to 4,784 input tokens ([vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)), so model perception belongs in a one-time indexing pass per file and only on regions that failed their gates.

## Two pipelines, one fallback order, and what to measure this week

### Vector PDF sheets

Each step runs only when the one before fails its gates. Start with a triage of each sheet in milliseconds: count extractable characters, vector paths, images, layers and annotations, and class the sheet as vector with text, vector with outline text, or raster. Find regions by rules and heading keywords, using layers where present. Rebuild each schedule from ruling lines on the crop with the grid builder or `find_tables(clip=...)`, resolve spanning cells and header paths, keep every cell rectangle, and export to Excel or SQLite with path-style column names. Run the validation gates, with Camelot lattice as the independent second reading.

The fallback order after a failed gate is: first, for a ruled table without usable text, look for AutoCAD's hidden SHX comments, then render the crop at 200 to 300 DPI, take the grid from Camelot lattice or img2table and OCR each cell with a CPU engine; second, for unruled or partly ruled tables, TATR or Docling's TableFormer on the crop with words from the PDF text layer; third, a cloud layout service on the crop at roughly USD 0.01 to 0.015 where data rules allow; last, a vision model on the crop under the acceptance rule above. Whole-sheet submission to any document parser, cloud service or vision model is not in the list.

### Photos and scans

A PDF with vector content bypasses this path entirely. For an image: classify flat scan against phone photo; run the cheap pre-gates on pixel size, saturation and sharpness and refuse early with a reason; detect the sheet or its printed border and rectify with a homography; test residual curvature on long lines and apply UVDoc only if needed, marking the geometry untrusted for measurement; correct shadows and keep coloured pen marks and stamps as a separate layer; detect text on tiles and apply the text-height gate; OCR rotated boxes with grammar validation; derive scale from a consensus of printed dimensions per viewport and ask for a two-point calibration when evidence is thin; extract room faces from linework, optionally comparing against a learned polygon proposal; and report with an overlay, never showing an area without its scale evidence. Schedules found in a photo go through the image branch of the PDF fallback order. A photo that fails a gate produces the reason and the retake instruction, not a low-confidence answer.

### Measurements that would replace the assumptions

Neither pipeline has been run. The following measurements, most of them a few hours each, replace the largest assumptions with numbers on the team's own sheets.

| Measure | How | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Triage class share | Count text, paths, layers and SHX comment annotations on every sheet in the set | How much the OCR and model fallbacks matter |
| Grid builder on the real failing finish schedule | Run it; compare to hand reading | Whether the column shift is fixed; first task from the earlier report |
| Cell-level accuracy of vector reconstruction | 10 to 20 sheets, every schedule cell hand-checked; correct cells over total cells, as in the Businessware test | Whether step three stands alone |
| Same metric for each fallback on the same crops | Camelot, TATR or Docling, one cloud service, one vision model | The real fallback order and cost per correct cell |
| Gate firing rate and precision | Count how often each gate fires on correct and on wrong tables | Which gates to keep; whether the shift alarm earns its place |
| Region finder recall | Schedules, legends and title blocks found over those present | Whether rules suffice or a detector is needed |
| CPU time per full sheet | Time triage, region finding and parsing on the 42 x 30 in sheets | Whether pdfplumber is too slow without object-level cropping |
| Paired photo test | Print or display 20 to 30 vector PDFs with known answers; photograph flat, oblique, shadowed and recompressed; run the same pipeline on both | The clean-to-photo degradation nobody has published, and the gate thresholds |
| OCR recall by text class | 30 to 50 sheets, scored separately on dimension strings, room labels and title block | Engine choice and tile size |
| Scale and area error | Consensus scale against the known scale; room areas against the vector PDF's areas | Whether photo measurements can be released at all |

## Conclusion

The research moves the question from "which tool replaces the model" to "which input class is this, and what evidence does it carry". A vector sheet carries exact lines and words, so reading it with code is both the cheap path and the accurate one, and the open risk is narrow: outline text and unusual layouts. A photo carries no exact data at all, and every published number that makes the tools look ready was measured on clean text pages or clean rasters. The honest product for photos is closer to what Bluebeam and Togal already sell, a proposal with visible evidence that a person confirms, than to an automatic answer; the refusal table is the feature, not an apology for it.

The team also holds something the literature lacks. Because many of its sheets exist as vector PDFs with computable ground truth, the paired photo test produces, in a day, the first measured degradation curve for photographed construction drawings and calibrates every threshold in this report at once. That small data set is worth more than any further tool survey, and it will show whether the triage share, not the choice of parser, is what decides how often a large model is called.
