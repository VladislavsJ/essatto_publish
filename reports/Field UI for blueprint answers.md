# Put the drawing before the number

The field UI should be a single phone page where every answer arrives as a large number with its unit, the Checked / Single source / Needs review label with one specific reason, and a thumbnail of the drawing with the counted or measured elements already marked, so that the evidence is seen without being asked for. The research is consistent on why: explanations help people catch AI errors only when checking is cheaper than accepting, and a numbered overlay on the drawing is the one piece of the existing product that makes a wrong answer look different from a right one. The step list and the label direct attention, but they do not verify anything by themselves. Technically this is a one-to-two-day build: a static HTML page served by the existing FastAPI app, Leaflet 1.9.4 in `CRS.Simple` mode for pinch-zoom, evidence shapes sent as JSON in image pixels and drawn client-side, and the base sheet sent as a small preview or as tiles, never as a full-resolution raster. For the hackathon, expose the machine that already runs the app through a Cloudflare named tunnel on an owned domain; for the first pilot, move the same Docker image to a Hetzner VM in Helsinki. Three limits apply throughout. No study has tested verification of numeric answers on drawings, so the design rules are transferred from other tasks. No sunlight or work-glove data exists for modern phones. Nothing here has been tested with a site engineer, and one hour with one is worth more than any further desk research.

## Sites punish small targets, dense toolbars and slow sheets

The phone on site is a tool for a quick check, not for browsing a drawing set. Fieldwire itself says a phone's screen makes it difficult to use as the primary means of accessing blueprints for most field crews ([Fieldwire](https://fieldwire.com/blog/equipping-the-field-to-go-digital)), and vendor guidance splits the roles: phone for fast updates and issue logging, tablet for drawings ([Ingenious.build](https://www.ingenious.build/blog-posts/best-construction-project-management-apps-ipad-iphone)). A question-and-answer app fits the phone role well because it takes the user straight to the relevant region instead of asking them to navigate.

The numbers on touch targets are much larger than consumer defaults. Nielsen Norman Group puts the floor at **1 cm by 1 cm** physical size and notes that walking or having hands full calls for bigger targets ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)). MIL-STD-1472, as reported in a small military study (n = 6), asks for **15 mm ungloved and 20 mm gloved**, and participants made no errors at those sizes with or without gloves ([touch zone study](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........9465c900e5780072de7a168d106fd14d)). Lab studies summarised by the W3C find accuracy levelling off around 20 mm ([W3C research summary](https://www.w3.org/WAI/GL/mobile-a11y-tf/wiki/Summary_of_Research_on_Touch/Pointer_Target_Size)). A 20 mm target is roughly 120 CSS pixels on a typical phone, so only one or two actions per screen can be that large. The practical rule that follows is a judgement, not a tested result: 48 CSS px as the floor for anything tappable, 56 to 72 px full-width buttons for primary actions in the bottom third of the screen, and no toolbars.

For sunlight there is physics but no tested threshold. Ambient light raises the display's black level and collapses contrast, and lightly coloured elements suffer most ([Grayhill](https://grayhill.com/blog/how-sunlight-readability-testing-ensures-clear-reliable-visual-interfaces/)). WCAG's 4.5:1 and 7:1 ratios are indoor figures ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). The safe reading is a light theme, text at 7:1 or better, heavy overlay strokes, and no meaning carried by colour alone. Connectivity is likewise poorly measured: no primary statistic on signal coverage was found, but Dalux sells "works offline, such as in a basement" ([Dalux](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/dalux-field/id504561520)), which shows where vendors see it failing.

### What existing field apps teach

The category has converged on one pattern: sheets split and named automatically, one "current set" with the revision visible, callouts hyperlinked between sheets, pins on the sheet tied to list items, and offline copies in native apps ([Fieldwire comparison, competitor-authored](https://www.fieldwire.com/blog/drawing-management-software/)). The same source reports the complaints: Procore and Autodesk Build are described by reviewers as complex and dense for people who need lightweight access, and field adoption as inconsistent. PlanGrid's newer viewer drew criticism for icons without labels ([TrustRadius, snippet-level reading](https://www.trustradius.com/compare-products/autodesk-build-vs-fieldwire-vs-hcss)). Bluebeam's own forum has users calling its Cloud app "not functional in the field", with files over a couple of megabytes stalling it ([Bluebeam Community](https://community.bluebeam.com/discussion/6172/bluebeam-cloud-not-functional-in-field)). Fieldwire limits offline to its native apps, not web ([Fieldwire comparison](https://www.fieldwire.com/blog/drawing-management-software/)). These sources are vendor forums and competitor summaries; Reddit, G2 and app-store reviews could not be read directly.

Three things carry over. The pin-tied-to-a-list-item pattern becomes numbered evidence marks tied to a list of counted items. The revision label becomes "computed from sheet A-102 rev C" on every answer. And the performance complaint becomes a hard rule: do not send a full sheet to a phone.

The adoption evidence says the software experience, not the hardware, decides use. In the 2018 FMI / PlanGrid survey (n = 599, vendor co-sponsored), people spent about **5.5 hours a week looking for project data**, and although more than 75% of firms issued phones or tablets to supervisors, fewer than one in five used apps consistently beyond email, text and calls ([PR Newswire](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/new-research-from-plangrid-and-fmi-identifies-factors-costing-the-construction-industry-more-than-177-billion-annually-300689826.html)). The same study names wrong or inaccessible data as a leading cause of rework. An answer tool that is occasionally wrong without saying so would worsen the problem it claims to solve.

### What a quantity needs beside it to be accepted

A number alone is not a quantity. National measurement rules differ on exactly the cases that change it. UK NRM2 sections for screeds and linings make no deduction for voids up to **1.00 m²** ([GCU teaching material](https://edshare.gcu.ac.uk/4066/2/index.html)); German VOB/C thresholds vary by trade, from 0.1 m² for tile and screed to 2.5 m² for wallpaper ([Bauprofessor](https://www.bauprofessor.de/aufmassregeln/)); DIN 277 separates gross area (BGF) from net room area (NRF) ([Bauprofessor](https://www.bauprofessor.de/din-277-grundflaechen-rauminhalte-hochbau/)), and SIA 416 does the same with different names. Every answer therefore needs four things in fixed order: the value with unit, what was measured in the user's words, the rule applied (net or gross, openings deducted or not), and the sheet and revision. The scale and how it was obtained belongs here too, since a wrong scale corrupts every area and length.

Vendor guidance on checking AI takeoffs describes the practice the UI should support: spot-check standard items, then verify exceptions, and look for wrong units, duplicate or missing counts and measurements that do not fit the plan ([ConstructConnect](https://www.constructconnect.com/blog/are-ai-takeoffs-accurate-in-2026)). The only tolerance figure found is the roughly 5% agreement between AI and manual takeoff that Togal cites from a peer-reviewed comparison ([Togal](https://www.togal.ai/case-study/peer-reviewed-study-togal-ai-vs-on-screen-takeoff)); no source gives an accepted site tolerance or rounding rule.

## Evidence builds trust only when checking is cheaper than accepting

The central finding from the human-AI research is uncomfortable for a product with a reasoning list. Bansal and colleagues found that explanations "increased the chance that humans accepted the AI's recommendation, regardless of whether it was correct" ([Bansal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)). Vasconcelos and colleagues (five studies, N = 731) showed why: people weigh the cost of verifying against the cost of accepting, and over-reliance falls when the explanation makes verification cheap ([Vasconcelos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)). Fok and Weld conclude that explanations help only to the extent they let a person verify the prediction ([Fok and Weld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07722)). Microsoft's guidance adds that explanations and the mere presence of sources raise trust even when they are wrong ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/ai/playbook/technology-guidance/overreliance-on-ai/overreliance-on-ai)).

Applied to the four things the product already has, this gives a clear ranking. The **highlighted drawing is the primary verification surface**: a missed door or a room outline through the wrong wall is visible at a glance. The reasoning steps are secondary, because wrong steps read as fluently as right ones. That ranking is an inference; no controlled study of answers over drawings or plans was found.

Two design consequences follow. First, evidence must not sit behind a link. NN/g reports that people rarely click citation links and that citations create false confidence ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/explainable-ai/)). The answer card should show the marked drawing by default. Second, the overlay must expose omissions, not only inclusions. For a count, every counted item gets a numbered badge, the last number equals the answer, and the user scans for an unmarked door. For an area, the measured region is filled so a missing or extra room is obvious. For a length, the traced path is drawn with segment lengths. Every number in the answer and in the steps should be tappable and should pan the drawing to the elements behind it; this is the drawing equivalent of MIT's SymGen, where linking each generated value to its source cell made verification about **20% faster** ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2024/making-it-easier-verify-ai-models-responses-1021)). A caution from the PaperTrail study applies on a small screen: mapping every claim to evidence cluttered the interface and hurt usability under time pressure ([PaperTrail](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21045v1)). Show one evidence layer at a time.

### The three labels are sound, with three repairs

Google's PAIR guidebook favours a few categorical levels over raw numbers, provided each level maps to a clear action, and warns that numeric confidence presumes users understand probability ([Google PAIR](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)). The product's labels are better still, because they describe what was done, not how likely the answer is. Keep them and do not add a percentage. Zhang and colleagues found that confidence displays calibrate trust but do not by themselves improve decision accuracy ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02114v1)), which is the reason the label must always sit next to something checkable.

The first repair is to state the action with each label. The second is to keep Needs review rare. A review of 23 clinical decision-support studies found alert override rates of **46.2% to 96.2%** ([Poly et al.](https://www.pubmed.gov/32706721/)); the transfer from medication alerts is an analogy, but the direction is clear. Track the share of answers flagged and the share of flags where the user changed something. The third repair concerns "Single source", which is the level most likely to be read as "fine" and is jargon that may translate poorly. Kim and colleagues (N = 404) found that direct first-person statements of uncertainty reduced over-reliance while impersonal hedges did not ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00623)). Whatever the label is called, its reason line should say plainly: "Nothing else on the drawing confirms or contradicts this."

| Label | Reason line (example) | Stated action | Layout |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checked | "Outline area matches the sum of room labels." | Use it. Look at the drawing if the number surprises you. | Number first, drawing thumbnail below |
| Single source | "Nothing else on the drawing confirms or contradicts this." | Compare with the drawing before you rely on it. | Number and thumbnail equal weight |
| Needs review | "Room labels add up to 54.1 m². Outline gives 55.5 m²." | Do not use until you have looked at the marked problem. | Failed check and region first, number secondary |

The reversed layout for Needs review is the one place friction is justified. Buçinca and colleagues (N = 199) showed that forcing people to engage reduces over-reliance but that users rate such designs least favourably ([Buçinca et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692)), so it should not be applied to every answer. Showing the two disagreeing values follows Si and colleagues, who found that presenting both supporting and refuting evidence reduced over-reliance ([Si et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12558)).

A calibration statement ("998 of 1,000 test questions with this label were right") belongs one tap down, with the sample and date. Stated accuracy raises trust, but accuracy observed in use overrides it ([Yin et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=569874)), so the figure must hold on the customer's own drawings or it will be discounted after the first visible miss.

### Explaining the computation without the word "AI"

Construction surveys put trust in accuracy at the top of stated barriers. Among 1,003 UK frontline workers, **58% trust AI to provide insights or instructions but not to make decisions** ([Builders Merchants News](https://www.buildersmerchantsnews.co.uk/news/merchants/survey-most-uk-construction-workers-trust-ai-automation-safe)); a 2025 survey of 400 North American professionals found 65% do not fully trust AI ([Placer Solutions](https://www.placersolutions.io/research-preview)). These are vendor-run surveys and directional only. They support positioning the product as "shows you where and what it counted", with the user owning the decision.

Generic AI labels lower trust, and saying specifically how AI was used softens the penalty; that evidence comes from news experiments and transfers only loosely ([Harvard Chan School](https://hsph.harvard.edu/health-communication/news/lessons-learned-transparency-around-using-ai-needs-to-be-specific-to-maintain-trust)). No study compares "computed by code" with "generated by a language model" for numeric answers. The recommended approach is therefore a reasoned choice: describe provenance by what was done, per part of the answer, in three fixed phrases: "Measured from the drawing geometry", "Read from text on the drawing", "Interpreted by AI from your question". One onboarding sentence carries the rest: the app measures the drawing the way a calculator adds numbers, so the same drawing always gives the same result, and where AI had to interpret something the app says so.

Two conditions keep this honest. NIST's explanation-accuracy principle requires that an explanation correctly reflect the process that produced the output ([NIST IR 8312](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2021/NIST.IR.8312.pdf)), and NN/g warns that step-by-step reasoning written by a language model is often unfaithful to how the answer was produced ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/explainable-ai/)). The steps shown must be generated from the actual computation trace, not written afterwards. And "computed" can raise trust beyond what accuracy justifies, because code computes exactly from detections that may be wrong. Pair the claim with its limit: "The measurement is exact for the outline shown. Check that the outline is the right one."

Wording should be built from fixed templates with numbers inserted, translated once with review. Practitioner guides agree on one idea per sentence, about 20 words at most, active voice and no idioms ([UK Home Office](https://hodigital.blog.gov.uk/2015/12/29/tips-for-writing-for-non-native-english-speakers/)). A step reads "Added the 5 rooms inside apartment B: 55.5 m²." A check reads "Room labels add up to 55.3 m². Outline gives 55.5 m². Difference 0.2 m². Pass." Probability words such as "likely" are read very differently by different people ([Willems et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09686)) and should not appear. Text on the drawing is never translated; Procore's interface list includes German and Polish but no Finnish or Swedish ([Procore](https://support.procore.com/faq/what-languages-are-available-in-procore)), so Nordic languages are an opening, with decimal commas and metric units from day one.

### Refusal and correction

A system that declines on unclear cases did not lose credibility in the one user study found ([Papenmeier et al.](https://api.deepai.org/publication/know-what-not-to-know-users-perception-of-abstaining-classifiers)), but repeated bare refusals send users elsewhere, as one participant in a 2026 qualitative study put it: "If you keep saying I don't know, I'm just going to go and use Google" ([Learning from AVA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17843)). A useful refusal says what could not be answered, the specific reason shown on the drawing, what partial result exists, and what the user can do next. It shows no number unless clearly marked partial, because a number on screen will be used.

Letting people adjust an algorithm's output raised their willingness to use it, largely regardless of how much adjustment was allowed ([Dietvorst et al.](https://repository.upenn.edu/marketing_papers/389)). Tap-to-remove a false door and tap-to-add a missed one, with the total updating at once, is the matching feature. After an edit the answer becomes "edited by you" and loses its Checked label unless checks are rerun. This is a pilot feature, not a hackathon one.

## Leaflet, pixel-coordinate JSON and tiles cover the viewer

For this build, **Leaflet 1.9.4 with `CRS.Simple`** is the lowest-effort viewer. It bundles pinch zoom, an image or tile base layer, tappable polygons, numbered markers and an animated fit-to-bounds in about 38 kB gzipped, from one script tag with no build step ([Leaflet releases](https://github.com/Leaflet/Leaflet/releases)). Use 1.9.4, not the 2.0 beta, which renames events and is still a prerelease. OpenSeadragon 6.x is the stronger deep-zoom engine, but interactive polygons need a plugin whose compatibility with version 6 could not be confirmed ([svg-overlay](https://github.com/openseadragon/svg-overlay)). `@panzoom/panzoom` at about 3.7 kB is enough only when the sheet fits in one safely sized image and no fit-to-rectangle animation is needed ([panzoom](https://github.com/timmywil/panzoom)). PDF.js in the browser should be avoided for large sheets; rasterise on the server. The Leaflet tutorial flags two traps: coordinates are `[y, x]`, and map units are not automatically image pixels ([Leaflet CRS.Simple](https://leafletjs.com/examples/crs-simple/)). Mapping pixel (x, y) to `L.latLng(-y, x)` keeps one map unit equal to one image pixel and avoids flipping polygons by hand.

The server contract should change from a baked PNG overlay to a clean base image plus shapes. A baked image cannot be tapped, blurs when zoomed and must be downloaded again for every answer. The server does every unit conversion once (DXF units, PDF points, scan pixels) and ships evidence in **full-resolution image pixels, origin top-left, y down**. Each evidence item carries an `id`, a display number `n` shared by the badge and the list row, a `label`, a `bbox` (always, for fly-to and for a minimum tap target) and an optional `polygon`. The sheet object carries width, height, image URL, a version hash for caching, and the affine from source units so real-world values can be shown on tap. This contract is a proposal from the research notes and has not been checked against the project's current response shapes. Keep the server-rendered PNG for the thumbnail, for sharing and as a fallback.

Small elements need help: a door on a 10,000-pixel sheet is a few CSS pixels at fit-to-screen zoom, so each gets a badge padded to about 44 px, and the same items are always reachable from the list. The two-way link is one map from evidence id to layer and row plus two functions: a row tap calls `flyToBounds` and flashes the shape; a shape tap scrolls the row into view. Skip the draggable bottom sheet. A column layout with the drawing on top, the list below at up to 45% of height, and a two-state collapse toggle gives most of the value for a fraction of the work.

### Large sheets and IFC models

Decoded bitmap memory is about width times height times four bytes. An A1 sheet at 300 dpi is about 70 megapixels, roughly 279 MB decoded, which no phone tab holds safely. Safari has long rejected canvases above **16,777,216 pixels** ([PQINA](https://pqina.nl/blog/canvas-area-exceeds-the-maximum-limit/)); a report that iOS 18 raised this is unconfirmed, and Apple publishes no figure. The working rule is a judgement: at or below about 12 megapixels, serve one WebP; above that, tile. libvips `dzsave` with `--layout google` writes a pyramid Leaflet can read, with the tile format set by a suffix option ([libvips](https://www.libvips.org/API/8.18/making-image-pyramids.html)). Vips writes `z/y/x`, so the Leaflet template must be `{z}/{y}/{x}`; verify on first run. In either case, show a preview of about 1,600 px on the longest side first. For the hackathon's 10 to 30 demo drawings, a downscaled single image per sheet is very likely enough and tiling can wait.

For IFC, generate a 2D storey plan on the server and highlight element footprints keyed by GlobalId in the same viewer. In-browser 3D of large models on a mid-range phone is not something to promise: That Open's Fragments format claims efficient display "on any device" but publishes no load times or mobile measurements ([That Open](https://github.com/ThatOpen/engine_fragment)), xeokit is AGPL-licensed ([xeokit](https://github.com/xeokit/xeokit-sdk)), and no phone benchmark for 10 to 300 MB models was found from any source. This is the largest evidence gap on the technical side.

### Tooling, offline and input

Plain HTML with ES modules and vendored Leaflet, served by FastAPI `StaticFiles`, needs no Node toolchain. Vendoring matters twice: a service worker can cache local files, and venue networks sometimes block third-party hosts. A manifest and a small service worker give installability and offline access to recent sheets and answers. iOS supports service workers and Cache Storage but not Background Sync, and has no install prompt ([firt.dev](https://firt.dev/notes/pwa-ios/)). WebKit evicts a whole origin's storage under pressure or after a period without interaction ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/14403/updates-to-storage-policy/)), so offline data is a cache, never the only copy. Questions are POSTs, which the Cache API does not store; make answers retrievable by GET at `/api/answers/{id}`.

Layout basics: size with `dvh`, respect safe-area insets, set `touch-action: none` on the drawing, and keep inputs at 16 px so iOS does not zoom on focus. Voice is an optional extra: the Web Speech API is only partially supported on iOS Safari and Chrome for Android ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/web-speech)), and the keyboard's own dictation key works with no code. Tappable example questions do more than a microphone button to reduce typing with cold hands.

Budgets are proposals, since no measured field-network guidance was retrieved: under 150 kB for the app shell, under 150 kB for the first view of a sheet, under 50 kB for answer JSON. Versioned image URLs get `Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable`; `index.html` and `sw.js` get `no-cache`.

## A tunnel serves the demo and a Helsinki VM serves the pilot

For the hackathon, keep the app where it already runs and publish it through a **Cloudflare named tunnel** on a domain already on Cloudflare. This gives a stable HTTPS hostname and needs only outbound access on port 7844 ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/connections/connect-networks/get-started/create-remote-tunnel/)). Tunnel is widely described as free, though the notes did not confirm this from a Cloudflare pricing page. If the domain is not yet on Cloudflare, nameserver changes can take hours, so start this first. The same-day fallback is a quick tunnel: one command, no account, but a random hostname that changes on restart, no uptime guarantee, a cap of 200 in-flight requests, and **no Server-Sent Events** ([Cloudflare Quick Tunnels](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/connections/connect-networks/do-more-with-tunnels/trycloudflare/)). Answers must therefore be plain JSON, not streamed. ngrok's free plan is unsuitable for an audience: 20,000 requests and 1 GB a month, and an interstitial warning page before HTML traffic ([ngrok](https://ngrok.com/docs/pricing-limits/free-plan-limits/)). Tailscale Funnel works but is beta with unpublished bandwidth limits ([Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/kb/1223/funnel)).

If the app runs on a university GPU machine, a tunnel publishes an internal service past the institution's firewall. No policy source was found; ask IT first, or precompute on that machine and serve from a laptop. Point the printed QR code at a redirect under the team's control so the backend can be swapped in seconds, and put the access code in the link.

Speed comes from the product's own property that most answers need no model call. Precompute answers and phone-sized images for the demo drawings so the request path is a lookup. Give every model call a 10 to 15 second timeout with the deterministic answer as fallback, cache model answers by drawing and normalised question, and warm the server before presenting. Keep a local screen recording of the full flow; organiser guidance is blunt that judges "score what they see on screen, not what almost happened behind it" ([Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/hackathon-demo-presentation-tips-pitch-3-minutes-win-2026/)). A chip on each answer card showing "0 model calls, 120 ms" makes the low-cost claim checkable by the audience.

Protection is mostly housekeeping. The model key stays in a server environment variable, in its own Anthropic workspace with a monthly spend limit ([Claude Platform docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/beta/organization/spend_limits.md)). The server accepts drawing IDs, never file paths. Rate limits apply per access code, since a venue shares one IP address. For the demo, disable uploads and use only the provided dataset.

A pilot with customer drawings needs paperwork even when small. Title blocks carry names and contact details, so assume personal data is present; GDPR Article 28 requires a written contract between controller and processor ([GDPR Art. 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/)). No regulator-level guidance was retrieved, so have this checked before it goes into a customer document. General B2B guidance, none of it construction-specific, asks for written success criteria and an end date before the pilot starts ([Causo](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/running-pocs-pilots-that-convert-2026)) and a named sponsor with budget plus a path to a paid contract ([Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/enterprise-pilot-to-paid-contract)). A reasonable primary criterion is accuracy on 50 to 100 of the customer's own questions, checked by their engineer, with zero confidently wrong answers. The ability to run with the model API switched off is a strong answer to "who sees our drawings".

## Six screens, built in two days by one developer

The app is one page with six states. Design every string with the longest target language, and put nothing tappable smaller than 48 px.

| Screen | What it shows | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Entry | Opened from a QR link carrying the access code. Project name, a list of sheets with number, title and revision, and three or four tappable example questions per sheet. No login, no tour. | Login friction and complexity are the recurring complaints; first use must work untrained. |
| 2. Ask | Sheet name and revision at top (read-only). Question input and a full-width Ask button pinned at the bottom; example questions as large chips above it; recent answers below. Typed text survives a failed request. | Thumb reach; gloves; basements. |
| 3. Answer card | In order: the value with unit as the largest element; one line saying what was measured and the rule applied ("Net floor area, apartment B, openings not deducted"); the label as icon, word and colour with its specific reason line and stated action; a thumbnail of the drawing with numbered or filled evidence; sheet, revision and time; a small provenance line and a "0 model calls, 120 ms" chip. Tapping the thumbnail or any number opens screen 4. | Evidence visible without a tap; label tied to an action; revision shown so a later drawing change is not read as an AI error. |
| 4. Evidence viewer | Drawing in the top 55% with pinch zoom; numbered badges or filled regions; collapsible list below with one row per item (number, label, value). Row tap flies to the item and flashes it; item tap scrolls to the row. For areas, the list is a short table of rooms and areas ending in the total. Scale and its source shown in the header. | The count check is "last badge equals the answer, no unmarked door"; the area check is the sum. |

## Conclusion

The research changes where the product's value sits. The reasoning steps and the label felt like the trust features; the evidence says they are the parts most able to raise acceptance of wrong answers, and that the plain overlay with numbered marks is what lets a foreman catch one. That makes the server's evidence geometry, not the prose, the asset to protect, and it makes "can a wrong answer be seen as wrong in five seconds" the test for every screen. It also implies two measurements to build in from the first deploy: how often each label's evidence view is opened, and how often a Needs review flag leads to a change. Few opens on Needs review means the label is being ignored; many on Checked means it is not believed.

What remains unknown is whether any of this holds for a person in gloves under a grey sky looking at their own drawing. No study covers that, and the construction surveys are vendor-run. The first pilot should be treated as the experiment the literature lacks: a fixed set of the customer's questions, a few planted wrong answers, and a count of how many the site engineer catches.
